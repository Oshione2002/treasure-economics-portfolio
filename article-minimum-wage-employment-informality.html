Nigeria’s ₦70,000 minimum wage: What changed in wages, employment and informality?

An empirical assessment of the 2024 reform using ten quarters of Nigeria Labour Force Survey microdata

On this page

Overview

Nigeria raised the statutory monthly minimum wage from ₦30,000 to ₦70,000 in 2024. The weighted share of valid monthly-paid wage employees earning below ₦70,000 subsequently declined, but that descriptive pattern does not by itself show that the reform caused the change. This portfolio project evaluates the reform using ten quarterly waves of Nigeria Labour Force Survey microdata and separates three questions: whether the below-₦70,000 share became smaller, whether more-exposed states experienced different post-reform changes in employment or informality than less-exposed states, and whether the identifying assumptions are strong enough to support a causal interpretation. That distinction is important because developing-country evidence shows that minimum-wage effects depend on how strongly the wage floor binds and how effectively it is enforced (Neumark & Munguía Corella, 2021).

Key findings

  • The weighted share of valid monthly-paid wage employees earning below ₦70,000 fell from 60.1% in 2024Q2 to 45.0% in 2025Q2.
  • The main exposure-based estimates compare post-reform changes in more-exposed states with changes in less-exposed states; they do not estimate the reform’s total national effect, and they do not show statistically clear differential changes in employment, unemployment, informality, self-employment, or usual weekly hours.
  • Formal wage employment and the wage-threshold outcome fail their relevant pre-trend diagnostics, so those post-period coefficients are not interpreted causally.
  • The share earning below ₦70,000 declined descriptively, but that threshold statistic alone does not establish reduced wage dispersion; a stronger causal evaluation requires direct implementation or enforcement data and a longer post-reform horizon.

The question behind the headline

A statutory wage increase can raise earnings without producing the same labour-market response in every setting. In economies with extensive informality, adjustment can occur through underpayment, changes in hiring, hours, self-employment, or movement between formal and informal work. Across Sub-Saharan Africa, minimum-wage outcomes are shaped by large differences in coverage and enforcement capacity (Bhorat et al., 2017). Nigeria is therefore an important case because the National Bureau of Statistics reported informal employment at 93.0 percent of total employment in 2024Q2 (National Bureau of Statistics, 2024a).

The research question is broader than whether average wages rose after July 2024. I test whether states with more monthly-paid wage employees below ₦70,000 before the reform experienced different post-reform changes in employment, unemployment, self-employment, informal employment, formal wage employment, usual weekly hours, and the low-wage share. This approach is motivated by evidence that formal and informal workers can respond differently to the same wage floor (Pérez Pérez, 2020).

Methodology and model specification

I used a quantitative quasi-experimental repeated-cross-section design. Ten public-use NLFS waves from 2022Q4 to 2025Q2 were harmonised at the individual level, producing 252,708 observations for people aged 15 years and above. National descriptive statistics are calculated directly from the weighted microdata. For the policy regressions, I then aggregate the microdata into outcome-specific state-quarter cells for Nigeria's 37 state-level units, including the FCT. Each state-quarter outcome is a survey-weighted mean or rate, and the regression weight is the sum of the NLFS survey weights contributing to that cell. The regressions are therefore weighted state-quarter models, not individual-level difference-in-differences regressions.

The continuous treatment variable is each state's weighted share of valid monthly-paid wage employees earning below ₦70,000 in 2024Q1-Q2. I define Exp10_s = 10 × Exposure_s, so a one-unit change represents a 10 percentage-point increase in pre-reform exposure. The main specification treats 2024Q3 as a transition quarter, excludes it from the baseline regression, and defines the post period as 2024Q4-2025Q2.

Baseline model:

Y_st = α_s + λ_t + β(Post_t × Exp10_s) + ε_st

Y_st is the survey-weighted outcome for state s in quarter t; α_s and λ_t are state and quarter fixed effects; and β is the exposure-based difference-in-differences coefficient. Standard errors are clustered by state. For rate outcomes, I report 100 × β, so the coefficient is the percentage-point outcome change associated with a 10 percentage-point increase in pre-policy exposure. For weekly hours, β is reported directly in hours.

Event-study diagnostic:

Y_st = α_s + λ_t + Σ_{k ∈ K, k ≠ −1} β_k [1(EventTime_t = k) × Exp10_s] + ε_st

The event study uses 2024Q2 as the omitted reference quarter and retains the actual missing 2023Q4 gap. I jointly test the pre-reform exposure interactions to assess the parallel-trends assumption. This diagnostic governs interpretation: when the pre-trend test fails, I describe the post-reform coefficient as an exposure-related association rather than a causal minimum-wage effect.

Robustness checks include an alternative specification that counts 2024Q3 as post, a model with state-specific linear trends, and a Q4-2024-only wage comparison that avoids the 2025 earnings-instrument change. I also estimate a youth triple-difference on state-quarter-age-group cells. The youth model adds Post_t × Exp10_s × Youth_g and includes state-by-age-group and quarter-by-age-group fixed effects; the triple-interaction coefficient measures whether ages 15-24 responded differently from older workers. All models use outcome-specific cell weights and state-clustered robust standard errors.

The earnings variable required separate harmonisation. Through 2024, the public-use NLFS records earnings in bands; for valid monthly-paid wage employees, bands 1-6 are below ₦70,000 and bands 7-9 begin at ₦70,000 or above. In the supplied 2025 files, exact earnings are reported for most respondents and the earlier band variable is used as a fallback when an exact amount is not reported. I harmonised these two measurement regimes explicitly before constructing the wage-threshold outcome.

What happened to the share earning below ₦70,000?

The most visible result is descriptive. Among valid monthly-paid wage employees, the weighted share earning below ₦70,000 falls from 60.1 percent in 2024Q2 to 55.1 percent in 2024Q3, 52.9 percent in 2024Q4, 48.5 percent in 2025Q1, and 45.0 percent in 2025Q2. The decline in this threshold statistic is economically meaningful, but the same series had already been declining before the reform. It also does not, by itself, demonstrate that overall wage dispersion narrowed. That pre-existing movement is why I do not treat the chart as a causal before-and-after estimate.

What the econometrics found

The main estimates compare post-reform changes in more-exposed states with changes in less-exposed states; they do not estimate the reform's total national effect. Per 10 percentage-point higher pre-policy exposure, the employment coefficient is 0.563 percentage points (p=0.239), unemployment is -0.215 percentage points (p=0.490), informality is -0.164 percentage points (p=0.717), and self-employment is -0.338 percentage points (p=0.374). Formal wage employment has an estimated coefficient of 0.190 percentage points (p=0.170), but its pre-trend test fails at the 5 percent level. Usual weekly hours fall by an estimated 0.536 hours (p=0.333). The data therefore do not show statistically clear differential post-reform changes between more- and less-exposed states for these broad labour-market outcomes over the available horizon.

The central result is not that the reform had no effect. The correct conclusion is that this state-exposure design does not detect a statistically clear differential effect on the broad labour-market outcomes. That distinction matters because developing-country studies report both limited employment responses and more adverse effects concentrated among workers or firms facing stronger compliance. For example, a South African study finds wage and poverty gains without detectable employment loss under weak compliance (Bassier & Ranchhod, 2024). A Colombian study finds stronger employment and hours reductions where compliance is higher (García et al., 2025).

Why I did not call the wage decline a causal effect

A causal difference-in-differences interpretation requires more- and less-exposed states to have comparable pre-treatment trends, conditional on the model. For the below-₦70,000 outcome, the joint event-study pre-trend test has p<0.001. The parallel-trends condition is therefore not supported for the wage outcome. Formal wage employment also fails its pre-trend test at p=0.039, so that post coefficient is not interpreted causally.

The practical implication is straightforward. Reporting only that the below-₦70,000 share fell from 60.1 percent in 2024Q2 to 45.0 percent in 2025Q2 would overstate what the design can establish if that decline were attributed to the reform. The defensible claim is that the share of valid monthly-paid wage employees earning below ₦70,000 declined during the reform period. The present state-exposure comparison cannot identify how much of that national descriptive decline was caused by the statutory increase, and the threshold statistic alone does not establish reduced wage dispersion. This caution is consistent with developing-country reviews showing that minimum-wage estimates are sensitive to bindingness, enforcement, and worker composition (Neumark & Munguía Corella, 2021).

What the evidence means

Three conclusions are supported by the evidence. First, the weighted share of valid monthly-paid wage employees earning below ₦70,000 declined materially around and after the reform period. Second, the exposure-gradient regressions do not show statistically clear differential post-reform changes between more- and less-exposed states in employment, unemployment, self-employment, informality, or usual weekly hours. These regressions should not be interpreted as estimates of the reform's total national effect. Third, the weak identification for the wage-threshold outcome means the descriptive decline and the causal policy effect must be kept separate. The broader literature suggests that this combination is plausible because wage gains can coexist with small employment effects when compliance is incomplete (Håkansta et al., 2025).

For policy analysis, the main lesson is that a national legal rate is not the same as effective treatment. Coverage, firm size, worker vulnerability, and enforcement determine whether a statutory increase becomes an actual wage increase or instead changes hiring, hours, or the form of employment. Turkey provides an example in which aggregate employment did not fall but informal employment increased among less-educated workers in small firms (Işık et al., 2020). A stronger Nigerian evaluation therefore needs dated implementation or enforcement information, ideally linked to employer or administrative records.

Research skills demonstrated

Microdata harmonisation
Combined ten NLFS quarters while documenting a 2025 earnings-instrument break and preserving survey weights.
Labour-market measurement
Constructed and validated employment, unemployment, informality, self-employment, wage employment and hours outcomes.
Quasi-experimental design
Specified and estimated a state-quarter continuous-exposure two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences model, dynamic event-study interactions, and a state-quarter-age-group triple-difference extension.
Diagnostic discipline
Tested pre-trends, alternative treatment timing, state-specific trends, same-instrument robustness and a youth triple-difference extension.
Reproducibility
Prepared harmonised Stata and CSV datasets, a data dictionary, results tables, figures and a built-in-command Stata do-file.
Research communication
Separated descriptive evidence from causal evidence and translated technical results into a policy-facing article.

Data and reproducibility

The project uses public-use Nigeria Labour Force Survey microdata from the National Bureau of Statistics. The reproducibility package contains the corrected Version 3 harmonised Stata and CSV datasets, a data dictionary, Stata-generated tables, labelled figures, and a do-file that recreates the reported outputs using built-in Stata commands. No user-written Stata package is required. Public-use data remain subject to the NBS access terms.
