Government spending and human capital development in Nigeria

A 1990–2022 study of whether public expenditure on education and health translated into measurable improvements in five human-capital outcomes.

FrequencyAnnual
MethodARDL / ECM

QuestionDesignEvidenceInterpretationLimitations

Research question

Does more spending become better human development?

Nigeria’s education and health budgets increased over time, but outcomes remained uneven. The study asked how total, recurrent and capital expenditure related to primary school enrolment, literacy, life expectancy, infant mortality and maternal mortality.

Why it matters

Budget growth is not enough on its own. The practical question is whether public expenditure reaches services and produces observable outcomes.

Research design

One spending system, five outcomes.

The empirical design used annual secondary data from the Central Bank of Nigeria, UNDP, World Bank and WHO-related sources. Augmented Dickey-Fuller and Phillips-Perron tests established integration properties before ARDL bounds testing and error-correction estimation.

InputsTotal, recurrent and capital expenditure on education and health
Education outcomesPrimary enrolment and literacy
Health outcomesLife expectancy, infant mortality and maternal mortality
ValidationNormality, heteroskedasticity, autocorrelation and specification checks

Reported evidence

The relationships were positive, but allocation quality remained decisive.

Education expenditureHigher enrolment and literacyreported positive relationship
Health expenditureHigher life expectancyreported positive relationship
Health expenditureLower infant and maternal mortalityreported negative relationship

The thesis reports a long-run relationship between public spending and human-capital measures. It also argues that inconsistent allocation, implementation weakness and inefficiency limited the gains.

Interpretation

The composition and delivery of spending matter alongside its size.

The results support sustained investment in education and health, but they do not justify treating every additional naira as equally productive. The policy argument is therefore two-part: maintain commitment to human-capital budgets, while strengthening transparency, equitable allocation and service delivery.

Limitations

What the model cannot settle.

  • The annual sample is relatively small and spans major political and economic changes.
  • Aggregate expenditure measures do not reveal how efficiently funds reached individual schools, hospitals or regions.
  • Estimated time-series relationships should not be read as definitive causal effects.
  • The thesis has not been peer reviewed or formally published.
