Domestic debt, external debt and economic growth in Nigeria

A disaggregated debt-growth study asking whether the source of government borrowing changes its short-run and long-run economic effects.

Research
collaboration

Period1981–2023
FrequencyAnnual
MethodARDL / ECM
CausalityGranger tests
Debt typesDomestic + external

On this page

QuestionDesignEvidenceInterpretationLimitations

Research question

Does it matter whether government borrowing is domestic or external?

Domestic debt avoids direct foreign-currency exposure but can compete with private borrowers for local funds. External debt can finance larger investments but increases exchange-rate and servicing risk. The study separated the two rather than treating total public debt as one variable.

Research design

Debt composition inside a broader macroeconomic system.

Annual data were analysed with ARDL and error-correction models, supported by stationarity and diagnostic tests. Inflation, interest rates, exchange rates and foreign direct investment were included to reflect the macroeconomic environment around borrowing.

Core outcomeEconomic growth
Debt variablesDomestic debt and external debt
ControlsInflation, interest rate, exchange rate and FDI
Direction testGranger causality

Reported evidence

Source and time horizon changed the result.

Domestic debtShort-run contraction

The economy adjusted over time, reducing the initial negative effect.

External debtConditional short-run support

Potential gains weakened when exchange-rate and servicing pressures accumulated.

Predictive directionBidirectional

Debt predicted growth and growth predicted later borrowing in the reported Granger tests.

Interpretation

Borrowing quality matters more than a simple debt label.

The findings do not support “domestic good, external bad” or the reverse. Productive use, fiscal discipline, currency exposure, financing cost and macroeconomic stability jointly shape whether debt supports or constrains growth.

Limitations

What the study could not observe.

  • Aggregate debt data do not show the productivity of each debt-financed project.
  • Government expenditure efficiency and political stability were not explicitly modelled.
  • ARDL/ECM models may miss nonlinear debt thresholds and structural breaks.
  • Granger causality is predictive and should not be interpreted as definitive structural causation.
  • The work has not been peer reviewed or formally published.
