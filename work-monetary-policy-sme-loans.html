THE IMPACT OF THE MONETARY POLICY RATE ON COMMERCIAL BANKS’ LOANS TO SMALL-SCALE ENTERPRISES IN NIGERIA

On this page

Abstract

This paper examines the effect of Nigeria’s Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) on commercial banks’ lending to small-scale enterprises over 1992–2023. Using annual data and an ARDL bounds-testing approach with an Error Correction Model (after standard descriptive, correlation and unit-root analysis), we assess both short- and long-run dynamics among log SME loans, MPR, prime lending rate, private-sector credit and inflation. The results indicate a long-run negative impact of MPR on SME lending: higher policy rates significantly reduce credit to small firms. This aligns with evidence that monetary tightening makes bank credit more expensive and less accessible to SMEs. Diagnostic tests confirm model validity. Policy implications are clear: raising the MPR can inadvertently squeeze SME finance, underscoring calls for supportive measures. We recommend targeted SME credit facilities to protect smaller enterprises and, when feasible, well thought out monetary easing to foster inclusive growth. These steps would help ensure that monetary policy promotes broad-based, SME-driven growth rather than constrains it.

Background

Access to bank finance is an important concern for small-scale enterprises. This article considers monetary policy in the context of lending to these businesses in Nigeria.

The discussion brings together policy rates, commercial lending conditions and wider private-sector credit, asking how their relationships should be understood rather than assuming a simple response to interest-rate changes.

What the article examines

The article examines the relationship between Nigeria’s Monetary Policy Rate and commercial-bank loans to small-scale enterprises over 1992–2023.

Its approach uses annual data and an ARDL specification that also considers prime lending rates, private-sector credit and inflation. The distinction between a contemporaneous association and a long-run response is central to interpreting the estimates.

Discussion and takeaways

The article considers how policy rates, wider credit conditions and lending to small enterprises relate to one another within its estimated model. A coefficient in the dynamic-levels equation cannot be treated as the normalised long-run response.

The reported estimates need further verification before a firm long-run lending effect can be stated. Credit access remains a policy consideration, rather than an effect established by these results.

Limitations

The original abstract and discussion reach conflicting long-run conclusions. Model stability and the long-run estimates require verification against the original estimation output; selected residual diagnostics alone do not resolve these concerns.

The annual sample and possible structural changes also limit interpretation. The results should not be read as proof that raising policy rates either increases or reduces small-enterprise lending.
